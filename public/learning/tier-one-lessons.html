L1.1 Who actually decides 2 min

Zoning isn't a form you file

There is no line on any application that reads "zoning approval." That single fact explains a startling number of wasted trips to City Hall.

Most people picture the process as a row of counters: get your zoning, then your permit, then your license. Three stops, three pieces of paper.

That isn't how it works. There is no separate zoning certificate to buy. Zoning review is a check that happens inside two other applications — and which one you meet depends on a single question: is anyone doing construction?

If the answer is no — you're moving into a space that's ready as it stands — the zoning question gets answered inside the business license application at the Department of Business Affairs and Consumer Protection. The city's Zoning division reviews it, and the license application can't be processed until that review clears. You never file anything with the Department of Buildings, because you aren't building anything.

If the answer is yes, the same question appears again, this time inside the building permit application at the Department of Buildings, where zoning is one of the reviews your plans pass through.

Same gate. Two doors. Which door you walk through is decided by the work, not by what kind of business you are.

This matters more than it sounds. People arrive at the Department of Buildings to "get their zoning" with no construction planned, and get sent away. Others clear zoning for a permit and assume licensing is handled. Neither is true, and both cost weeks.

Check yourself

You're opening a shop in a space that needs no construction work at all. Where does the zoning question get answered?

B. There is no separate zoning certificate to obtain. Every business license application is reviewed by the Zoning division before it can be processed. The Department of Buildings is the construction path — with no construction, you have no business there.

L1.2 Who actually decides 3 min

Three kinds of decision, three sets of manners

Calling your alderman about a project is ordinary politics. Calling a Zoning Board member about your pending case is prohibited. That is not a difference in etiquette. It's a difference in what kind of decision each one is making.

Chicago's land use bodies fall into three families. Once you can sort them, most of the confusing rules stop being confusing.

Legislative bodies make the rules. City Council and its zoning committee change the zoning map and adopt ordinances. They are elected, or answerable to the elected, and they are meant to hear from the public — that's the job. Your alderman belongs to this family.

Administrative staff apply the rules to facts. The Zoning Administrator reads the ordinance against a specific property. Plan examiners check drawings against code. Licensing staff process applications. None of them are deciding what the rule ought to be; they're determining what it already says.

Quasi-judicial bodies decide individual cases on a record, the way a court does. The Zoning Board of Appeals is the one most people meet. It hears exactly three kinds of matter: requests for variations, requests for special use approval, and appeals arguing that the Zoning Administrator got it wrong. That third one is routinely left out of popular explanations, and it's the one that matters when you believe staff misread the ordinance.

That classification is where the rules come from. Because the Board decides cases rather than sets policy, it takes testimony, builds a record, and issues written resolutions. And its own Rules of Procedure prohibit ex parte communication — contact concerning the merits or procedural posture of a case pending before it. Not because members are unfriendly, but because a decision resting partly on what one side said privately isn't a decision on the record.

Questions still get answered. They go through the department staff who administer the process, rather than the people who will vote.

Check yourself

Which of these may be contacted about a matter currently pending?

B. Legislative officials are meant to hear from constituents. The Zoning Board is quasi-judicial, and its rules prohibit communication about a pending case's merits or posture. Route those questions through department staff.

L1.3Who actually decides3 min

What each body hands you

"We're approved" means at least four different things depending on who said it. The fastest way to know where a project actually stands is to ask what piece of paper exists, and who signed it.

Trace the paper.

A zoning clearance isn't a certificate you hold. It's a review that happens inside another application, and the way you know it happened is that the application moves forward. Nothing gets handed to you, which is exactly why people think this step was skipped.

A Zoning Board resolution is a written decision, signed by the chair, including findings of fact that explain why an application was approved or denied. It resolves a zoning question. It does not authorize anyone to build anything.

A building permit authorizes construction. Inspection sign-offs confirm that what was built matches what was permitted. A Certificate of Occupancy, where one is required, makes occupying the building lawful. A business license authorizes operating.

Six documents, four issuers, none interchangeable. The most common and most expensive confusion is treating a zoning approval as permission to build, or a completed inspection as permission to open.

Check yourself

You hold a Zoning Board resolution approving a special use for your site. May construction begin?

B. The resolution settles the zoning question. Construction is authorized by a building permit from the Department of Buildings, which is a separate application to a separate department.

L1.4Who actually decides2 min

Why a stranger knows about your permit

A week or two after you file, an email arrives. It demands payment of a permit fee to avoid a delay. It cites your address, describes your project, and quotes your application number correctly.

It is not from the city.

The FBI has issued a public warning about criminals impersonating city and county officials to collect fraudulent payments for planning and zoning permits. They find their targets in publicly available permit information — the same records that make the development process transparent also make everyone with an open application findable, along with what they are building and what stage they're at.

That's the part worth sitting with. The details in the message are not evidence that the sender is legitimate. They're evidence that the sender can read a public database. Accuracy is the cheapest thing for a scammer to obtain here, which is precisely why it feels so convincing.

The pattern has three parts: specific knowledge of your project, urgency — a deadline, a threatened delay, a lapsing approval — and a payment method supplied in the message itself. Any legitimate fee you owe will be payable through a channel you can find independently, on a department's own published page.

So the rule is short. Never pay, click, or reply using contact information that arrived in the message. Look up the department yourself and ask them. A real obligation survives that phone call; a fake one doesn't.

Check yourself

An email cites your correct application number and demands immediate payment to prevent a delay. What does the correct application number tell you?

B. Permit application details are public record. Knowing them demonstrates only that the sender can read public data. Verify through the department's own published contact information, never the message's.

L2.1Zoning and permitted uses3 min

Read the table, not the rumor

The listing says "zoned for retail." The broker says the last tenant did something similar. The previous owner says it was never a problem.

None of those is a source.

Getting to a real answer takes three moves, in this order.

Identify the parcel. A street address is a convenience, not an identifier. Addresses get shared across buildings, split, renumbered, and misreported. The Property Index Number — the PIN — is how the county identifies a specific piece of land, and it's what you want when you're about to spend money based on the answer.

Find the district. Every parcel sits in a mapped zoning district, and the official zoning map is what says which. Not the listing, not a screenshot, not a third-party site working from an old copy.

Translate your business into a use category. This is where most people go wrong. The ordinance doesn't organize itself by what businesses call themselves. It organizes by use category — its own classification for activities.

One trap worth naming: what the previous tenant did tells you very little. They may have been operating under a special use approval that doesn't travel with the space, or as a nonconforming use tied to circumstances that ended when they left. Inheriting a storefront is not inheriting its permissions.

Check yourself

Your business calls itself a "juice bar." What do you look up in the ordinance?

B. The use tables are organized by use categories defined in the ordinance, not by trade names. What the previous tenant did may reflect approvals or conditions that do not transfer with the space.

L2.2 Zoning and permitted uses 3 min

Four symbols, four futures

One character in one cell decides whether you open in two months or two years. It's worth knowing how to find it.

The zoning ordinance answers "can I do this here?" with a table. Down the side are use categories — the ordinance's own classifications for activities, which is why you look up what your business is, not what it calls itself. Across the top are zoning districts. Find your row, find your column, and the cell where they cross holds one of four characters.

P — permitted by right. The use is allowed in that district. Note what this does not mean: it doesn't mean no approvals. Every other standard in the ordinance still applies. It means you don't need special use approval.

S — special use. Allowed, but only after review and approval under the special use procedures at § 17-13-0900. This routes you to the Zoning Board, which considers whether the use fits this particular location. An S is not a no. It's a longer yes.

PD — planned development. Allowed only through the planned development process at § 17-13-0600. This one is commonly misunderstood as a size threshold — something that kicks in only for large projects. It isn't. PD is its own designation in the table, and where the table says PD, it says PD no matter how small the project is.

– — prohibited. Expressly not allowed. A dash feels like the worst outcome, and it is frequently the most valuable one you can get. A dash found in week one saves you the months that a dash found in month six costs.

The discipline is simple: the table is the authority. Not a listing description, not a broker's summary, not whatever the last tenant was doing. Those are all guesses about what the cell says. Go read the cell.

Check yourself

Your use shows "PD" in your district, and yours is a small project. What does that tell you?

B. PD is a use-table designation, not a size threshold. Where the table assigns PD to a use, that process applies at any scale.

L2.3Zoning and permitted uses3 min

Three different asks to one board

People say "I'm going to zoning" as though it were one thing. It's three, and walking in with the wrong one is a way to lose months.

A special use asks whether a use the ordinance already contemplates for your district belongs at this particular location. The table said S. The use isn't forbidden — it's conditional, and the condition is compatibility with what's around it.

A variation asks for relief from a standard. The rule exists, you can't meet it, and you're asking for it to apply differently here. Notice the difference in posture: a special use argues this fits; a variation argues this rule works badly on this parcel.

An appeal argues that the Zoning Administrator got it wrong. You are not asking for permission or relief. You are contesting a determination, and the question is what the ordinance actually says.

Sorting your situation into the right one of these three is most of the work. It determines what you have to prove, what evidence is relevant, and whether you're asking for a favor or asserting a right.

Check yourself

Staff tells you your use isn't allowed in your district. You believe they read the use table incorrectly. What is that?

C. You aren't seeking permission or relief — you're contesting a determination. Appeals of the Zoning Administrator's decisions are one of the Board's three jurisdictions.

L2.4Zoning and permitted uses3 min

What a hearing actually is

Speakers are sworn in by the chair. Witnesses can be questioned by the other side. Board members ask whatever they want, whenever they want. The decision arrives as a signed written document containing findings of fact.

That is a courtroom, not a meeting. And once you see it that way, every rule that seemed strange starts making sense.

Everyone who speaks is identified and sworn. The chair administers oaths and can compel witnesses to attend. You are giving testimony, not offering an opinion.

Both sides get to examine witnesses. A public hearing carries the right to appear and present evidence, and also the right to hear and question the witnesses the other side brings.

The decision is written and reasoned. The chair signs a written decision including findings of fact, and the Board keeps minutes recording how each member voted.

Now the trap, which costs people their participation more often than anything else in this lesson: if you want to present evidence or cross-examine witnesses, you must register with the Board in advance. There's a deadline that runs before the hearing date. Miss it and you can still attend and watch — but you've given up standing to participate as a party.

That deadline is the reason neighbors sometimes arrive prepared and articulate on the day and still can't be heard the way they expected. Nobody is being unfair to them. They filed nothing, and this is a proceeding on a record.

Check yourself

You intend to cross-examine a witness at an upcoming hearing. What must have happened first?

B. Participants who want to present evidence or cross-examine must register with the Board ahead of the hearing date. Check the current deadline in the Rules of Procedure — attending is open, participating as a party is not.

L3.1Permits and opening2 min

The cheapest lesson: when you need no permit

The fastest permit is the one you don't need. A limited range of nonstructural repairs, minor alterations, some site work, and non-occupied structures require no building permit at all — and finding out that your project is in that range is the single cheapest five minutes available to you.

There's a principle underneath the list, and it's worth carrying even when the details change: the line is drawn at structure and escape.

Work stays outside the permit requirement when it doesn't touch what holds the building up or how people get out of it. Interior finishes are the clearest case — carpet, hardwood, tile, paint, wallpaper. You're changing surfaces, not the building.

Work crosses into requiring a permit once it cuts away or removes part of an exterior wall, an interior wall or partition, a subfloor, or a roof; removes or cuts a structural beam, column, or load-bearing support; or removes or changes a required means of egress.

Read that list again and notice it's really two ideas. The first several items are structure — the things that keep the building standing. The last is egress — the path people use to get out in an emergency. Those are the two things the building code exists to protect, so those are the two things that trigger review.

One caution. A contractor telling you "you don't need a permit for that" is not a source, and the consequences of being wrong land on the owner, not the contractor.

Check yourself

Which of these crosses the line into requiring a building permit?

B. The boundary is structure and egress. Removing part of an interior wall or partition is exactly the kind of work that triggers a permit requirement; surface finishes generally do not.

L3.2Permits and opening3 min

Four tracks, and what sorts you into one

Most guides to Chicago permitting name three tracks. One of the three they name was discontinued in November 2023.

That's worth knowing for its own sake, and worth knowing as a warning: program names look like stable structure and aren't. Here are the four current application programs.

What sorts you into a track is the nature and complexity of the work, not your preference or your hurry. A project needing architectural plans is not going to fit through a repair-oriented track because you'd like it to move faster.

The useful habit here isn't memorizing the four names. It's recognizing the shape: a light track for repairs, a professional-certification track, a full-review track, and a complex-project track. That shape has been stable across renamings. The names haven't.

Check yourself

Which program is the main process for a permit application that requires architectural plans?

B. Standard Plan Review is the main track for applications requiring architectural plans. Express covers repairs and small improvements; Developer Services is aimed at moderate to complex projects.

L3.3Permits and opening2 min

Self-certification is a liability transfer

Self-certification is usually described as a way to skip plan review. That description is accurate about the mechanics and completely wrong about what's happening.

Nobody waived anything. Someone signed for it.

Under the Self-Certified Permit Application Program, the design professional of record — an Illinois-licensed architect or structural engineer — takes full responsibility for code compliance. They stamp the drawings and certify that the plans comply with the Chicago building code. On that basis, the department forgoes conducting its own plan review.

The code did not get easier. The review did not get skipped. What changed is who is answerable if the plans turn out not to comply, and that shift is where the speed comes from.

Two consequences follow. Not every project qualifies — eligibility is defined by the program, and wanting to move faster doesn't create it. And not every architect will do it: a professional certifying compliance is accepting exposure they would otherwise share with a plan examiner. If a designer is reluctant, that's a considered professional judgment about risk, not obstruction.

Check yourself

Under self-certification, who is responsible for code compliance?

B. The design professional of record certifies that the stamped plans comply, and the department forgoes its own plan review on that basis. The requirement didn't change — the responsibility moved.

L3.4Permits and opening3 min

Permit, occupancy, license: three finish lines

Finishing construction is not the same as being allowed to occupy the space. Being allowed to occupy the space is not the same as being allowed to operate a business in it. Three finish lines, three issuers.

Here is the part almost every popular guide gets wrong: not every project requires a Certificate of Occupancy. It is required for permitted work falling into specific categories — a building with enough dwelling or sleeping units, non-residential work above a floor-area threshold, a change of occupancy, or work on assembly, educational, institutional, or hazardous occupancies.

So "get your Certificate of Occupancy and open your doors" is simply not the story for many small tenant build-outs. Plenty never require one at all.

Getting this wrong hurts in both directions. Assume you need one when you don't and you wait for a document nobody is going to issue. Assume you don't when you do and you have a real problem: where a certificate is required, it is unlawful to occupy the building, or let anyone else occupy it, before obtaining that certificate — or a partial or temporary one.

Watch the change-of-occupancy trigger especially. Converting a space from one kind of use to another can put you in scope even when the space is small and the work looks modest.

Check yourself

Does every commercial build-out end with a Certificate of Occupancy?

B. The requirement is triggered by categories — unit counts, floor area, change of occupancy, and certain occupancy types — not by every project. Where it does apply, occupying before obtaining it is unlawful, so confirm which side of the line your project falls on.
